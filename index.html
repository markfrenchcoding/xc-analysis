How this works

The short version

Chute plays the rest of the season out, from here to the finish at Lane, and then does it again. Thousands of times. Every league meet, every selection decision, every state race. The numbers on the board are simply how often each thing happened.

A season is not one race

Line every runner up at their season best and you learn who is fastest on paper. You do not learn how likely anything is, and paper does not run in November.

Runners have days

Nobody races the same twice. Some days the legs are there and some days they are not. Chute treats that as the main event, because on race day it usually is.

Two things about those days matter more than people expect. The first is that teammates tend to have them together. The same bus, the same week of training, the same course that either suits a squad or does not. The second is the shape of the days themselves.

One good afternoon is not a season

One perfect afternoon in September tells you less than a season of racing does. Where a runner has more than one result, Chute reads the whole season instead of only its best line.

Checked against seasons we already know

Anyone can publish numbers. The question is whether they hold up, so we checked.

We rebuilt four past seasons exactly as they stood eight weeks before their own state meet, with nothing after that date and no hindsight, then ran each season forward and compared it to what actually happened at Lane in November.

It is easy to sort teams. It is hard to be right about how sure you are, and that is the part worth checking. The whole scorecard is on the Track record tab above, written straight out of the harness: what we got right, what we got wrong, and where the odds are still too confident. A model that has never been scored is only an opinion.

One limitation, stated plainly. Those checks were run on 6A, which is where the history is. Every classification runs on the same engine and the same data, and every qualifying rule comes straight from OSAA: how many teams a league sends, how many at-large places exist, and the fact that only four runners score for 3A and 2A/1A girls. But 2026 is the first year of four-runner scoring, and last season the small-school girls raced as one combined division, so there is no matching history to test those two boards against yet. Treat 6A's numbers as evidence about the method rather than as a measured accuracy for every board.

What-ifs you can trust

Drop five seconds off a runner and the board moves. The trick is making sure it moved because of the five seconds and not because the dice rolled differently, so every comparison is run against identical races. What you see is the improvement, with the luck held still.

What it does not know

Courses, only partly. Every race is rated by how the runners in it ran against their own form, so a slow day on a hilly course counts for more than the same time on a fast one. It cannot tell a hard course from bad weather on the same afternoon, and a race nobody raced twice around cannot be rated at all.

The future, in detail. We do not know who gets injured, who has a breakthrough, or who joins the squad in October. We do know roughly how much that matters, because we measured it against four past seasons, so the board widens its spread by a fixed amount that depends only on how far away Lane is. It is an allowance for surprise, not a prediction of one. Early-season numbers are still a shape rather than a promise, and they sharpen on their own as November gets closer.

The committee. At-large selection here is a sensible stand-in for a room full of people who also weigh league strength and head-to-head.

We would rather say all of this up front than have you find it later.
