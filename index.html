The seven leagues

Two teams out of each league go straight to Lane. Everyone else is chasing two at-large spots.

How this works

What goes in

Every 6A athlete's marks from athletic.net, grouped by school, seven fastest per team. A team needs five runners to score; teams short of five sit out and are named on the Leagues tab. The 5,000m and 3,000m boards are scored separately — nothing is converted between them, because team places come from finishing order and a 3,000m race produces order just as well as a 5,000m one.

Sampling an athlete

A runner is not reduced to their best day. Each race draws from their top three marks — 25% best, 50% second, 25% third. With fewer than three marks the weights renormalise over what exists, so a single mark is used every time.

Race-day noise

The drawn mark is then scaled by 1 + team shock + individual noise. Thirty percent of the variance is a shock shared by all seven of a squad — travel, tactics, a course that suits them. Variances add, so the two are √0.30 and √0.70 of the dial. Independent draws would cancel inside a five-runner sum and make every favourite look safer than they are.

Individual noise is right-skewed. A good day is capped by fitness; a bad one — illness, a fall, going out too hard — is not. The slow half is stretched ×1.45 and the fast half compressed ×0.75, then recentred and rescaled so the dial still means one standard deviation.

A meet-wide shock is deliberately absent: multiplying everyone in a race by the same number cannot change the order of finish.

The two fair courses agree almost exactly: Lane runs about 1% slower, and race-to-race spread is roughly 2.25%. That is where the default comes from. Ultimook was dropped — Hydrangea Ranch is effectively an obstacle course, and that 4.4% measured the terrain rather than the athletes.

The two fair references are six weeks apart and give the same ratio, which says something useful: across the back half of the season, fitness gains and Lane's extra difficulty roughly cancel. The ratio itself never enters the simulation — it is uniform, so it cannot change order. Only the spread does.

Scoring a race

NFHS rules. Runners are placed by time, a team's first five finishers sum to its score, and runners six and seven take places without scoring — they displace rivals. Ties break on the sixth runner, and a team without a sixth loses any tie it is in. Teams that cannot field five are removed before places are assigned, so their runners displace nobody.

Getting to Lane

Two teams from each of the seven leagues qualify automatically — fourteen berths. Third-place teams enter the at-large pool, ranked here by their scoring five's average time. A fourth-place team is only eligible once its own league's third has been taken, which is the OSAA rule and is why fourth place almost never gets in with only two spots. Two at-large teams are chosen, for a sixteen-team field.

The state meet is then run with a fresh set of draws. A team that got hot at districts starts again from its marks, which is the whole point of running the season thousands of times rather than once.

What if

Both worlds are scored against the same simulated races, so the difference you see is the improvement and not the luck. When several runners are adjusted, each is also run on its own to show what it is worth alone — and comparing the sum of those to the combined figure tells you whether the gains stack or overlap.

The solver adds five seconds at a time to whichever runner buys the most, re-probing after each step, and stops once the outcome is more likely than not. It gives up at 180 seconds across the squad.

What it does not know

Course difficulty is not modelled — a mark from a flat park and one from a hilly farm are treated alike, which is the largest source of error this early in the season. Nobody improves, gets injured, or changes the top seven between now and November. The at-large ranking is a stand-in for a committee that also weighs league strength and head-to-head. And a league with only two scoring teams will show both at 100%, which is arithmetic rather than a prediction.

What if a runner improves?

Take time off one or more of your seven and see what it does to your league and state odds. Both worlds are run against the same simulated races, so the difference is the improvement, not luck.
