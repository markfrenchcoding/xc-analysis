The seven leagues

Two teams out of each league go straight to Lane. Everyone else is chasing two at-large spots.

How this works

The short version

Chute plays the rest of the season out, from here to the finish at Lane, and then does it again. Thousands of times. Every league meet, every selection decision, every state race. The numbers on the board are simply how often each thing happened.

A season is not one race

Line every runner up at their season best and you learn who is fastest on paper. You do not learn how likely anything is — and paper does not run in November.

Runners have days

Nobody races the same twice. Some days the legs are there, some days they are not, and the model treats that as the main event rather than an afterthought.

Two things about those days matter more than people expect. The first is that teammates tend to have them together — the same bus, the same week of training, the same course that suits them or does not. The second is the shape of the days themselves.

A best time is not a whole season

One perfect afternoon in September tells you less than a season of racing does. Where a runner has more than one result, Chute uses the shape of their season rather than the single best line of it.

Checked against seasons we already know

This is the part we would want to see if we were you.

We rebuilt the database exactly as it stood in mid-September of past seasons — nothing after that date, no hindsight — ran the simulation forward, and compared it to what actually happened at Lane in November. Not just whether the right teams were picked, but whether the percentages meant what they said: of all the teams given a one-in-four chance, did about one in four make it?

That is where the settings come from, and it is how we know which parts of this are sharp and which are still guesswork. A model nobody has scored is just an opinion with decimals.

What-ifs you can trust

Drop five seconds off a runner and the board moves. The trick is making sure it moved because of the five seconds and not because the dice rolled differently — so every comparison is run against identical races. What you see is the improvement, with the luck held still.

What it does not know

Courses. A flat park and a hilly farm are treated alike, and that is the biggest gap. We have measured it and we are working on it.

The future. Nobody gets injured, nobody has a breakthrough, and the top seven in September are the top seven in November. Real seasons are less tidy, so the further out you are, the more the true spread exceeds what any model shows. Read early-season numbers as a shape, not a promise — they sharpen as the season fills in.

The committee. At-large selection here is a sensible stand-in for a room full of people who also weigh league strength and head-to-head.

None of that is hidden in the small print, because the honest version is more useful than the confident one.

What if a runner improves?

Take time off one or more of your seven and see what it does to your league and state odds. Both worlds are run against the same simulated races, so the difference is the improvement, not luck.
